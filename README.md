1.	Introduction

The relationship between language and selfhood has occupied philosophers, psychologists, and neuroscientists for more than a century, yet the specific phenomenon of egocentric speech in the adult population has received comparatively limited systematic attention within clinical and neuroscientific literature. Jean Piaget's foundational accounts of

egocentric language positioned it as a feature of early childhood cognition, a developmental stage characterised by the child's incapacity to adopt a perspective other than their own in communicative acts (Piaget, 1923). Within Piagetian developmental theory, egocentric speech is expected to diminish as the child internalises language and acquires the cognitive capacity for sociocentric communication. However, clinical observation across the fields of psychoanalysis, psychotherapy, and psychiatry has long identified analogous speech patterns persisting into, or emerging distinctively within, adult life — patterns not reducible to developmental failure but constitutive of a qualitatively different and clinically significant phenomenon.

It is within this context that the theoretical and clinical contribution of Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo, a Medical Doctor and Analytical Psychotherapist based in Clifton Park, New York, acquires its significance. In formulating what has come to be known as the Bumbalo Model of Egocentric Language in the Adult, Dr. Bumbalo has reconceptualised adult egocentric speech as a symbolic, regulatory, and identity-organising function rather than as a deficit or pathological residue (Bumbalo, 2026a, 2026b). The Bumbalo Model situates ELA at the intersection of Jungian depth psychology, clinical phenomenology, relational trauma theory, and the psychology of identity formation, positioning it as a psychic regulatory mechanism that, under conditions of identity stress or relational threat, commands the architecture of adult speech in recognisable and clinically tractable ways.

The present article seeks to extend and deepen the Bumbalo Model by situating it within the methodological and conceptual framework of neurophenomenology, as originally articulated by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991) in their landmark work on the embodied mind, and further developed through Northoff's spatiotemporal neuroscience of selfhood (Northoff & Huang, 2017; Northoff & Ventura, 2025). Neurophenomenology proposes that first-person experiential accounts and third-person neuroscientific data operate as mutually constraining sources of evidence, such that neither can be reduced to nor replaced by the other. This bidirectional methodology offers a uniquely appropriate framework for theorising ELA, which presents simultaneously as a phenomenologically

rich experiential event — available through first-person report, clinical observation, and depth psychological interpretation — and as a presumed neurodynamic process occurring within identifiable neural network configurations.

The aims of this article are threefold. First, to articulate the core theoretical architecture of the Bumbalo Model and its grounding in Jungian depth psychology and clinical phenomenology. Second, to review the neuroscientific literature pertaining to the neural networks most relevant to self-referential language production, focusing on the Executive Control Network, the Limbic Network, and the Narrative Default Mode Network. Third, to synthesise these two domains within an original tripartite neurophenomenological model of ELA, one that accounts for both the neural underpinnings and the symbolically mediated experiential character of adult egocentric speech, and that carries direct implications for clinical practice.



2.	Theoretical Background

2.1	The Bumbalo Model of ELA

As proposed by Bumbalo (2026a, 2026b), Egocentric Language in the Adult constitutes a speech mode qualitatively distinct from the developmental egocentrism described by Piaget (1923) and from the self-promotional discourse associated with clinical narcissistic pathology. Within the Bumbalo Model, ELA is defined as a persistent and clinically observable pattern in which an adult's communicative output is organised, often without conscious deliberate intent, around the central axis of the self. Discourse threads are not merely coloured by self-reference but are structurally compelled toward it; every conversational departure eventually returns to the speaking subject as both origin and terminus of meaning. Crucially, this centripetal speech structure is not conceived as mere egoism or social inconsideration but as a psychic regulatory mechanism — a mode by which the self attempts to establish, consolidate, or restore its coherence under conditions of psychological threat.

The Bumbalo Model roots this regulatory function in Jungian depth psychology, drawing specifically on Jung's (1969) theorisation of the Self as the organising archetype of the psyche, the shadow as the repository of disavowed or split-off psychic content, and individuation as the lifelong developmental process through which these are integrated into a coherent identity structure. Within this framework, as Bumbalo (2026a) proposes, ELA may be understood as a linguistic manifestation of the Self's regulatory activity under conditions of threat, dissociation, or identity fragmentation. The speech act becomes, in essence, a symbolic act: a reaching toward coherence through narrative, imagery, and the affective resonance of self-referential language.

The Bumbalo Model further identifies relational trauma as a principal aetiological context for ELA. When early relational experiences have produced conditions of insecure attachment, chronic misattunement, or explicit relational traumatisation, the developing self acquires patterns of identity consolidation that are markedly self-referential, compensating through speech for the regulatory failures of the relational environment. In adult life, these patterns persist as ELA — not as static deficits but as dynamically activated regulatory strategies, particularly in contexts of interpersonal stress, perceived rejection, or identity threat (Bumbalo, 2026b). The Bumbalo Model is thus careful to distinguish ELA from pathological narcissism as classically defined in psychiatric nosology. While ELA and narcissistic personality disorder may share phenomenological surface features — particularly the centralising of the self in discourse — ELA is positioned within the Bumbalo Model as a broader clinical phenomenon, not reducible to a personality disorder category, and present across a wide range of clinical presentations including anxiety disorders, dissociative conditions, relational difficulties, and existential crises of identity.

2.2	Neurophenomenology as a Methodological Framework

Neurophenomenology, as systematically developed by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991), proposes a methodology for the study of mind that operates through the mutual constraint of first-person phenomenological description and third-person cognitive neuroscientific investigation. Departing from both eliminative materialist and purely

phenomenological approaches, neurophenomenology insists that subjective experience and neural process are not reducible one to the other but stand in a relation of reciprocal illumination. First-person accounts — structured through rigorous phenomenological method — constrain and guide the interpretation of neural data, while neuroscientific findings impose constraints on phenomenological theory, ensuring its empirical tractability.

This methodological commitment provides a particularly apt framework for the study of ELA, given that the phenomenon is simultaneously phenomenologically rich and presumed to be neurally grounded. The clinical encounter with ELA-presenting individuals furnishes abundant first-person and observational data concerning the experiential character of egocentric speech, its emotional urgency, its symbolic texture, and its identity-regulatory function. Neurophenomenology provides the conceptual architecture to bring these first- person data into productive dialogue with neuroscientific investigation without reducing the one to the other.

Northoff's spatiotemporal neuroscience constitutes a significant recent development within this methodological tradition. Northoff and Huang (2017) proposed that consciousness and its varied dimensions — including self-referential processing — are best understood as arising from the brain's intrinsic temporal and spatial dynamics, its spontaneous neural activity organised across multiple timescales. This temporo-spatial theory of consciousness (TTC) provides a framework within which the self is understood not as a static entity but as a dynamically enacted process, continuously elaborated across the brain's intrinsic temporal architecture (Northoff & Huang, 2017). Northoff and Ventura (2025) have more recently advanced a programme of convergent neurophenomenology that explicitly bridges spatiotemporal neuroscience and first-person experience, providing resources for the kind of integration proposed in the present article.

Central to any neurophenomenological account of ELA is the Default Mode Network (DMN). The DMN, first systematically characterised by Buckner, Andrews-Hanna, and Schacter (2008), comprises a distributed set of cortical regions — principally the medial

prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, and angular gyrus — that are preferentially active during internally directed cognition, including autobiographical memory retrieval, prospection, social cognition, and narrative self-elaboration. The DMN has been extensively implicated in self-referential processing; its activity is reliably observed when individuals engage with personally relevant material, construct self- narratives, or reflect on their own mental states (Buckner et al., 2008; Spreng, Mar, & Kim, 2009). It represents, in neurodynamic terms, the principal substrate of what Gallagher (2000) has theorised as narrative selfhood — the self as constituted through the ongoing elaboration of a temporal narrative of personal identity.

2.3	Neural Networks Relevant to Self-Referential Language
Three principal neural network systems are of direct relevance to the production and regulation of ELA. The first is the Executive Control Network (ECN), anchored bilaterally in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). The ECN performs top-down regulatory functions including cognitive inhibition, attentional allocation, working memory maintenance, and response selection (Sui & Humphreys, 2015). In the context of language production, ECN engagement is associated with the capacity to regulate the content and direction of speech in accordance with contextual demands, social conventions, and communicative goals. When ECN function is intact and appropriately engaged, it provides the regulatory architecture that prevents unconstrained self-referential elaboration from dominating communicative output.

The second network is the Limbic Network, whose principal components — the amygdala, orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and hippocampus — subserve affective processing, emotional memory consolidation, and the assignment of salience to self-relevant stimuli. The amygdala is critically involved in threat detection and the generation of affective responses, particularly in the context of social and relational stimuli; the OFC mediates the integration of affective valence with ongoing cognitive and communicative processes; and the hippocampus is essential for the encoding and retrieval of autobiographical memories that form the content of self-referential narrative (Damasio, 1999). Limbic network activation is

reliably associated with heightened self-referential processing, particularly under conditions of perceived threat, loss, or relational disruption. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have consistently demonstrated co-activation of the DMN and limbic network during tasks involving self-referential processing (Yan, 2024).

The third network is the Narrative Default Mode Network, as described above. Spreng et al. (2009) demonstrated that a common neural basis underlies autobiographical memory, prospection, theory of mind, and navigation, all of which are unified by their engagement of DMN circuitry in the service of constructing coherent, temporally integrated, self- relevant mental simulations. This narrative function of the DMN is the neural substrate of what the Bumbalo Model addresses at the symbolic and clinical level: the compulsive construction of self-narrative as a regulatory and identity-consolidating activity.



3.	A Neurophenomenological Model of ELA

3.1	The Tripartite Neural Architecture of ELA

The tripartite neurophenomenological model proposed here conceives ELA as emerging from a characteristic pattern of dynamic tension and co-regulatory failure among the three neural network systems identified above: the ECN, the Limbic Network, and the Narrative DMN. Under conditions of psychological equilibrium, these three networks operate in a state of dynamic balance. The ECN provides top-down regulatory oversight of internally generated self-referential content, preventing its unconstrained elaboration into communicative output; the Limbic Network provides appropriate affective salience to self- relevant stimuli without overwhelming the regulatory capacity of the ECN; and the Narrative DMN elaborates autobiographical content and self-narratives in a temporally integrated and contextually calibrated fashion, enabling what Gallagher (2000) terms the narrative self to function coherently in social and communicative contexts.

ELA, within the present model, is proposed to emerge when this tripartite equilibrium is disrupted through a specific neurodynamic sequence. In the first phase, an identity-

threatening or relationally activating stimulus — whether external (interpersonal conflict, perceived rejection, social evaluation) or internal (intrusion of traumatic memory, existential anxiety, identity uncertainty) — produces limbic hyperactivation. The amygdala registers the affective salience of the stimulus; the OFC begins integrating this affective signal with self-relevant content; and the hippocampus begins cueing autobiographical memories associated with prior experiences of analogous threat. This limbic hyperactivation constitutes the neurodynamic triggering condition for ELA.

In the second phase, the amplified limbic signal propagates across functional connectivity pathways to the ECN, producing the paradoxical suppression of executive regulatory capacity that is characteristic of high-arousal states. Research on the neurodynamics of stress and affective dysregulation has established that amygdala hyperactivation exerts inhibitory effects on prefrontal regulatory circuits, particularly the DLPFC, compromising the ECN's capacity to provide top-down regulation of internally generated content (Damasio, 1999; Sui & Humphreys, 2015). In the context of language production, this ECN suppression means that the normal regulatory filtering of self-referential elaboration is reduced or suspended: the cognitive mechanisms that would ordinarily constrain the extent to which self-referential content drives communicative output are functionally compromised.

In the third phase, with ECN regulatory suppression in place, the Narrative DMN receives an amplified limbic signal and a reduced regulatory constraint simultaneously. The consequence is the release of unconstrained narrative elaboration: the DMN's self- referential and autobiographical processing proceeds without the top-down modulation ordinarily imposed by ECN oversight, generating a compulsive, centripetal, and symbolically saturated stream of self-referential speech. This is, within the present model, the neural substrate of ELA as described within the Bumbalo Model (Bumbalo, 2026a, 2026b). The neurodynamic account thus offers a third-person, mechanistic correlate for the first-person and clinical observations that constitute the Bumbalo Model's empirical

basis, while the Bumbalo Model furnishes the phenomenological depth and symbolic interpretive framework that gives the neural account its clinical meaning.

It is important to note that this neurodynamic account does not imply a linear or deterministic causal chain. Consistent with the neurophenomenological methodology of Varela et al. (1991) and the spatiotemporal framework of Northoff and Huang (2017), the three networks are understood as dynamically co-regulating systems whose interactions are characterised by reciprocal influence, bidirectional modulation, and context- sensitivity. The ECN-Limbic-DMN dynamic underlying ELA is not a fixed circuit but a probabilistic neurodynamic configuration that is more or less likely to emerge depending on the individual's relational history, current affective state, identity stability, and the specific features of the interpersonal context.

3.2	The Symbolic Mediation Layer

The tripartite neural account presented in Section 3.1 provides a necessary but not sufficient explanation of ELA as conceptualised within the Bumbalo Model. A purely neurodynamic account risks reducing ELA to a mechanistic process, eliding the phenomenological richness, symbolic complexity, and clinical significance that constitute its defining features within depth psychological theory. To address this, the present model proposes a symbolic mediation layer that operates between the limbic affective signal and the narrative DMN output, and that is theorised through the integration of Jungian symbolic theory with the neuroscience of meaning-making.

Within Jungian depth psychology, as systematised in Jung (1969), the psyche is organised around a series of archetypal structures — the Self, the Persona, the Shadow, the Anima/Animus, among others — that represent universal patterns of psychic organisation, individually instantiated through personal experience and expressed through symbolic content including imagery, narrative, and language. These archetypal structures are not merely metaphorical; they constitute the organising principles of psychic life, investing experiential  content  with  affective  charge,  narrative  directionality,  and  symbolic

significance that transcend the merely personal. As proposed by Bumbalo (2026a), ELA is not simply a symptom of affective dysregulation but a symbolic act: the speaking self is activating archetypal complexes — above all, the complex of the Self — in its attempt to restore psychic coherence under conditions of threat or fragmentation.

This Jungian symbolic account finds a compelling correlate in Damasio's (1999) neuroscientific theory of somatic markers and the role of affect in meaning-making. For Damasio, the body's affective responses — somatic markers — provide the evaluative valence through which cognitive content acquires meaning and motivational salience. The limbic system, as the neural substrate of somatic marking, thus does not merely generate emotional arousal but actively participates in the constitution of meaning. When limbic hyperactivation drives ELA, it does not flood the narrative DMN with contentless affective noise; rather, it invests narrative content with the affectively saturated somatic markings that constitute, in the Jungian framework, the activation of archetypal symbolic material.

Gallagher's (2000) account of narrative selfhood further supports this integration. Gallagher distinguishes between a minimal, pre-reflective bodily self and a narrative self constituted through the temporal elaboration of autobiographical experience into a coherent identity story. The narrative self is not a fixed entity but a process of continuous symbolic elaboration, achieved through language, memory, and interpretive activity. Within the present model, ELA represents an intensification and compulsive iteration of this narrative self-constitutive process, driven by limbic activation and released from ECN constraint, and saturated with the archetypal symbolic content that Jungian depth psychology identifies as the grammar of psychic self-organisation (Jung, 1969). The symbolic mediation layer is thus the functional interface between limbic affect and narrative speech, the site at which somatic markers become symbols, and symbols become self-referential speech.

3.3	ELA in Clinical Context: Phenomenological Markers

Consistent with the neurophenomenological commitment to the mutual constraint of first- person and third-person data, the present model identifies three phenomenological markers of ELA that are directly derivable from the tripartite neural account and consistent with the clinical observations embedded in the Bumbalo Model. These markers do not constitute a diagnostic checklist but are offered as theoretically grounded phenomenological signatures that may guide clinical recognition and interpretive engagement.

The first phenomenological marker is centripetal speech structure. In ELA, discourse is organised around the speaking self as its structural centre of gravity; all thematic departures — however they are initiated, whether by the clinician's intervention, by an ostensibly other-directed inquiry, or by the emergence of apparently external subject matter — eventually return to the self as origin and terminus of meaning. This centripetal quality reflects, within the tripartite model, the dominance of narrative DMN elaboration unconstrained by ECN regulatory oversight: the narrative network's intrinsic bias toward self-relevant content, ordinarily balanced by executive regulation, is given unmediated expression (Buckner et al., 2008; Spreng et al., 2009).

The second phenomenological marker is affective urgency. The emotional charge carried by ELA speech consistently exceeds what is warranted by the informational content of the utterance; there is a palpable quality of necessity, of pressure, in the speech act. This affective urgency is the phenomenological expression of the limbic hyperactivation that, within the tripartite model, both triggers and sustains ELA. The amygdala-driven affective signal, registered somatically and invested symbolically through the mediation layer, lends the speech act a quality of existential imperative: the speaking self does not merely communicate but appears to be regulated by its communication (Damasio, 1999; Yan, 2024).

The third phenomenological marker is symbolic overdetermination. ELA speech is characterised by the repeated deployment of particular metaphors, images, and narrative motifs that carry a density of meaning disproportionate to their apparent surface content.

These recurring symbols — which in Jungian terms represent the activation of archetypal complexes — organise the self-narrative into a coherent symbolic structure, one that is not consciously designed but appears to arise from the depths of psychic organisation (Jung, 1969). Within the tripartite model, symbolic overdetermination reflects the activity of the symbolic mediation layer: limbic affect is not merely expressed but symbolically elaborated, generating the affectively saturated archetypal imagery that gives ELA its characteristic depth and clinical richness, as consistently observed within the Bumbalo Model (Bumbalo, 2026a, 2026b).



4.	Discussion

4.1	Implications for Depth Psychological Practice

The tripartite neurophenomenological model of ELA carries several significant implications for clinical practice within depth psychological and psychoanalytic frameworks. Most immediately, it reframes limbic hyperactivation — frequently presented in clinical settings as a management problem, a resistance, or an obstacle to reflective therapeutic work — as a primary therapeutic entry point. If ELA is neurodynamically rooted in limbic activation that has exceeded ECN regulatory capacity, then the clinical priority is not the suppression of ELA but its careful phenomenological reception. The affective urgency of the ELA-presenting client signals a state of identity threat or relational stress in which the self's regulatory resources have been mobilised to capacity; the therapeutic task is to receive this signal with sufficient attunement and interpretive skill that the client's ECN regulatory capacity can be gradually restored through the co- regulatory function of the therapeutic relationship.

Furthermore, the model supports the clinical use of symbolic content as primary interpretive material. Within the Bumbalo Model, ELA narratives are not communicative failures to be corrected but symbolic acts to be interpreted. The tripartite model grounds this insight neurodynamically: the symbolic overdetermination characteristic of ELA

reflects the activation of the symbolic mediation layer, through which limbic affect is invested with archetypal content. This content, precisely because it is symbolically organised rather than consciously designed, provides privileged access to the Jungian unconscious, to the complexes, defences, and identity structures that organise the client's psychic life below the level of reflective awareness (Jung, 1969; Bumbalo, 2026a). The depth psychological clinician who receives ELA with this interpretive orientation will find in it a rich symbolic text, rather than a communicative obstacle.

The model also enables a clinically significant neurodynamic distinction between ELA and pathological narcissism. Both phenomena present with self-centred discourse and reduced receptivity to the other's perspective; however, at the level of neurodynamics, they are distinguishable. Narcissistic pathology is associated with structural deficits in mentalising capacity and empathic processing, implicating relatively fixed alterations in prefrontal- limbic connectivity. ELA, by contrast, is conceptualised within the Bumbalo Model and the present tripartite account as a dynamically activated state, responsive to contextual triggers of identity stress and amenable to co-regulatory therapeutic intervention. This distinction has important implications for treatment planning, prognosis, and the form of therapeutic engagement indicated (Bumbalo, 2026b).

4.2	ELA, Trauma, and Identity Regulation

The Bumbalo Model positions relational trauma as a central aetiological context for ELA (Bumbalo, 2026a, 2026b), and the tripartite neurophenomenological model provides a neural architecture through which this claim can be specified and extended. Relational trauma, particularly when experienced early in development and within the context of primary attachment relationships, produces well-documented effects on the neurodevelopment of the limbic system, the ECN, and their functional connectivity. Chronic relational trauma is associated with amygdala hyperreactivity, reduced hippocampal volume and autobiographical memory coherence, and compromised prefrontal regulatory capacity — precisely the configuration that the present model identifies as the neurodynamic substrate of ELA (Damasio, 1999; Yan, 2024).

The consequence of this trauma-related neurodynamic configuration is a structural vulnerability to ELA: the traumatised individual's tripartite regulatory system is characterised by a lower threshold for limbic hyperactivation, a reduced ECN regulatory reserve, and a narrative DMN that has been shaped by traumatic autobiographical content. When relational stressors activate this system — as they reliably do in adult interpersonal life, particularly in the context of therapeutic relationships — the tripartite system is more readily tipped into the ELA configuration than in individuals without significant relational trauma histories. The result is that ELA is particularly prevalent and clinically salient in populations presenting with relational trauma, dissociative conditions, and the complex identity disturbances that constitute the contemporary epidemic of psychological suffering that the Bumbalo Model specifically addresses (Bumbalo, 2026b).

Of particular significance is the role of dissociation in ELA phenomenology. Within the Bumbalo Model, identity dissociation — the partial or intermittent fragmentation of the self-structure consequent upon relational trauma — is a key context for ELA production. The tripartite model provides a neural account of this relationship: dissociation is associated with disrupted integration across the DMN's narrative self-construction circuits, resulting in the loss of the temporal coherence that ordinarily organises autobiographical memory into a continuous self-narrative (Buckner et al., 2008; Northoff & Huang, 2017). ELA may thus function, in dissociative states, as a compensatory attempt to restore narrative coherence: the compulsive centripetal structuring of discourse around the self represents the psyche's effort to reintegrate what dissociation has fragmented. Understanding this function is essential for the clinician who might otherwise experience ELA as a therapeutic impediment rather than as a self-regulatory communication of considerable depth.

4.3	Limitations and Future Directions

The tripartite neurophenomenological model of ELA proposed in this article is explicitly theoretical in character. Its neural claims are derived from the extrapolation and integration of established neuroscientific findings regarding the ECN, Limbic Network, and

DMN, applied to the specific clinical and phenomenological phenomenon of ELA as theorised within the Bumbalo Model. No primary empirical neuroimaging or electrophysiological data specifically pertaining to ELA-producing individuals have been collected or analysed in the preparation of this article; no clinical statistics or empirical data have been fabricated. The model's empirical validity accordingly awaits prospective testing, and the authors are careful to present its propositions as theoretically motivated hypotheses rather than established findings.

The most urgent empirical priority generated by the present model is the design of studies combining neurophenomenological methodology with contemporary neuroimaging and electrophysiological measures. Specifically, fMRI paradigms incorporating experience- sampling methods and structured first-person phenomenological reporting — consistent with the neurophenomenological methodology of Varela et al. (1991) and the convergent approach of Northoff and Ventura (2025) — could be employed to investigate the functional connectivity patterns and network dynamics associated with ELA-type self- referential speech production in clinical populations. EEG studies focusing on the temporal dynamics of network co-activation during spontaneous self-referential speech would complement these findings with high temporal resolution data concerning the sequence of ECN suppression and DMN release posited by the present model.

A critical methodological challenge for such studies is the operationalisation and stratification of ELA as a participant variable. The Bumbalo Model provides a theoretically coherent and clinically grounded taxonomy of ELA features — centripetal speech structure, affective urgency, and symbolic overdetermination — that offers a candidate framework for the development of validated clinical assessment instruments. The development of such instruments, grounded in the Bumbalo Model's phenomenological markers and subjected to formal psychometric validation, would represent a significant methodological contribution enabling the empirical programme outlined above. Future interdisciplinary collaboration between depth psychological clinicians, neurophenomenologists, and cognitive neuroscientists is therefore strongly indicated, with the Bumbalo Model

providing an ideal clinical taxonomy for participant stratification and interpretive framing (Bumbalo, 2026a, 2026b).



5.	Conclusion

This article has proposed a tripartite neurophenomenological model of Egocentric Language in the Adult, integrating the clinically and theoretically foundational Bumbalo Model with contemporary neuroscience of selfhood and the philosophical methodology of neurophenomenology. ELA is theorised as emerging from a characteristic neurodynamic configuration in which limbic hyperactivation suppresses executive regulatory function, releasing unconstrained narrative DMN elaboration into self-referential speech saturated with symbolically mediated archetypal content. The symbolic mediation layer — articulated through the integration of Jungian depth psychology, Damasio's somatic marker theory, and Gallagher's account of narrative selfhood — ensures that the model accounts not only for the neural mechanics of ELA production but for its phenomenological richness, its clinical significance, and its function as a psychic regulatory and identity- organising activity.

Dr. Bumbalo's contribution in formulating the ELA framework represents a clinically grounded, symbolically sophisticated, and theoretically productive advance in the understanding of adult self-referential speech. By positioning ELA within a neurophenomenological framework, the present article demonstrates that the Bumbalo Model is not only clinically tractable but empirically testable, possessing the theoretical coherence required for integration with neuroscientific investigation. The implications of this integration extend across clinical practice, depth psychological theory, and the neuroscience of selfhood, and invite the kind of sustained interdisciplinary engagement that the complexity and contemporary clinical significance of ELA fully demands.
